# JS8 — The Mode

Outcome: by the end of this chapter you
can say what a JS8 transmission
physically is — tones, timing, and error
correction — and what the four speeds
trade against each other. The numbers
quoted are the program's own, read from
its Mode menu in the lab (t05).

## Tones in a sideband channel

JS8 is an **8-FSK** mode: eight audio
tones, one sounding at a time, the
sequence of tones carrying the data. The
tones are generated by the computer's
sound card and fed to an ordinary
single-sideband transmitter, so on the
air a JS8 signal is a narrow warble
inside a normal SSB channel — roughly
the width of a fraction of a voice. A
station's signal sits at an **offset**:
a position in hertz above the dial
frequency, inside the receiver's
passband. The lab's window sat at
1500 Hz offset on a 14.078 MHz dial
(t03), which the API later confirmed
arithmetically: dial 14 078 000 Hz plus
offset 1500 Hz equals the working
frequency 14 079 500 Hz (t04).

The receiving side runs the process
backwards, continuously: digitise the
audio, slide it through banks of
filters, and hunt for the distinctive
pattern of a JS8 frame starting at one
of the frame boundaries.

## Frames, and the clock that rules them

JS8 sends in **frames** — fixed-length
transmissions that begin on strict UTC
boundaries. This is why chapter 4 calls
your computer's clock radio equipment:
if your clock is seconds out, your
frames start at the wrong moment and
nobody's decoder, listening at the
boundaries it expects, will line up
with you. Operating systems that sync
to network time handle this quietly;
the point is to know it matters.

Inside a frame, the text is not sent as
letters. It is compressed, wrapped in
**forward error correction** (FEC) —
extra mathematical structure, in JS8's
case inherited from FT8's low-density
parity-check design — and spread across
the frame's symbols along with
synchronisation patterns the decoder
uses to find the frame in the noise.
The FEC is the magic trick: the decoder
can lose a substantial share of the
frame to static and still reconstruct
the exact text, and it *knows* when it
has succeeded, because the frame carries
checks the reconstruction must satisfy.
A decode is not a guess. Either the
checks pass and the text is right, or
there is no decode.

That is also how JS8 reaches its famous
sensitivity. Depending on speed, a JS8
frame can be decoded at signal-to-noise
ratios around **−24 dB** and below — the
signal is some two hundred times weaker
than the noise in the same bandwidth,
and inaudible to any ear. The program
reports each decode's SNR so you can
watch it happen.

## The four speeds

One size does not fit all conditions, so
JS8Call offers four frame lengths. The
Mode menu names them with their vital
statistics (t05):

| Speed | Frame | Width | Typing speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow | 30 s | 25 Hz | ~8 WPM |
| Normal | 15 s | 50 Hz | ~16 WPM |
| Fast | 10 s | 80 Hz | ~24 WPM |
| Turbo | 6 s | 160 Hz | ~40 WPM |

The trade is exactly what the table
suggests. A Slow frame spends half a
minute painting the same text into the
noise, so it survives the worst
conditions and takes the least spectrum
— and delivers text at reading-aloud
pace. Turbo shouts the text in six
seconds and needs a far stronger signal
to survive. Normal is the default
meeting point and the speed most
activity uses. Crucially, the **MULTI**
decoder option — on in the lab's window
— decodes all four speeds at once, so a
listening station need not guess which
speed the band is using tonight. A
conversation can also change speed
mid-stream: start in Normal, drop to
Slow when the band fades, step up to
Fast when it recovers.

Longer texts simply take more frames.
A message of a few words fits one
Normal frame; a paragraph becomes a
short train of them, sent back to back
while the receiving decoder reassembles
the sequence. This is why JS8 feels
like slow chat rather than telegraphy:
you type a thought, and half a minute
later it has arrived, entire.

## What JS8 is not

It is not encryption — every station in
range decodes everything, by design and
by law. It is not fast — even Turbo is
a focused typist's pace. And it is not
private in any sense: your callsign is
in every frame, which is rather the
point of amateur radio. Chapter 15
collects the rest of the limits,
including the ones belonging to this
guide's lab rather than to the mode.
